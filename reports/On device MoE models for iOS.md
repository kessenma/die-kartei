# Streamed MoEs fit iPhones, not your tutor

**No realistic path yet puts a bigger, more capable German tutor on your iPhone or iPad than the fine-tuned Gemma 4 E4B, even though larger Mixture-of-Experts models now do run on phones.** Flash-streaming engines keep only the dense core and a few active experts in RAM and read the rest from NAND: Edge0's iPhone app, open-sourced on 30 September, decodes a 4-bit 35B-A3B at **6.4 tok/s** on an iPhone 16 Pro, and an independent study measured **1.8–3.1 tok/s** for the same model class on an iPhone 17 Pro Max. Every such engine prefills at only 5–7 tok/s, needs a ~20 GB download per model, and has no published German results, and none can carry your fine-tune, because Edge0's routing predictor and recovery adapter are trained against one specific base and upstream MLX has declined the mmap work that streaming needs. Your own training data adds a deeper objection: as a data teacher, the ~4B-active Gemma 4 26B-A4B wrote correct hard-case reflexive errors only **12%** of the time against **100%** (of non-empty rows) for the dense 31B, so its large total parameter count bought fluency but not the subtle grammar reasoning a tutor runs on, and the MoEs small enough to sit in RAM have just 1–1.5B active parameters, below your measured capacity floor. The useful next moves are therefore cheap measurements rather than a model swap: read the real per-app memory ceiling on both devices (your docs estimate ~7–8 GB on the 17 Pro, a June 2026 forum report measured a flat ~6.1 GB), score stock Gemma 4 26B-A4B and the dense 12B on your guarded suites for a few dollars before paying for any fine-tune, and test whether a memory-mapped llama.cpp build of the existing E4B can take ~4 GB of weights out of the 8 GB iPad's jetsam footprint without losing the tune.

## Flash-streamed 35B MoEs run on iPhones, slowly and without your tune

A Mixture-of-Experts model uses only a few of its experts for each token, and the 2026 engines exploit that: they keep attention, the router and the shared experts in RAM and read routed experts from flash on demand, so peak memory tracks the active set rather than the parameter count ([Edge0 GitHub](https://github.com/Edge0-AI/Edge0)). Four projects have now done this on a real iPhone, and their numbers set expectations for everything that follows.

### Edge0 has shipped an iPhone app since 30 September

Edge0 is an Apache-2.0 engine by Yu Lin and four coauthors (the company behind it is unconfirmed), first released on 8 September 2026 with two 4-bit checkpoints: **Edge0-35B-A3B**, built on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and run with 4 routed experts per token where the base uses 8, and **Edge0-8B-A1B**, built on inclusionAI's Ling 3.0 tiny ([Edge0 GitHub](https://github.com/Edge0-AI/Edge0); [HF Edge0-8B-A1B](https://huggingface.co/Edge0/Edge0-8B-A1B-preview)). Two trained add-ons make it work. A per-layer "prerouter" predicts the next layer's experts one token ahead and is used as the routing itself, so flash reads start before they are needed, which the project credits with up to **+59% decode** ([arXiv 2609.18063](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18063)). A "Recover-LoRA", distilled from the full-precision teacher onto the frozen int4 base, wins back what quantization and the replaced routing cost.

The notes disagree on platform support, and the newer primary source settles it. Mid-September coverage described Edge0 as macOS/MLX only and called the iPhone claim unverified ([runtimewire](https://runtimewire.com/article/edge0-open-source-35b-model-streaming-inference); [mer.vin](https://mer.vin/news/edge0-runs-a-35b-parameter-moe-model-in-under-3gb-of-ram/)), but the repo's own timeline records iOS (Swift + MLX Swift), Android and Windows engines open-sourced on **2026-09-30**, and its iOS README publishes iPhone 16 Pro measurements ([Edge0 ios/README](https://github.com/Edge0-AI/Edge0/tree/main/ios)). **Edge0 runs on iPhone today**, as a Developer-Mode sideload that copies weights into the app bundle: about 5 GB for the 8B, and about 20 GB for the 35B after a repack that turns each expert's nine scattered reads into one sequential read. Those iPhone numbers are the project's own, taken on an iPhone 16 Pro with no prompt length, memory or thermal state stated; nobody has published iPhone 17 Pro figures, and the widely quoted 15–20 tok/s came from a 24 GB Mac mini M4 Pro, not a phone.

These results do not carry equal weight. Routide's paper, by an independent researcher, is the only controlled characterization on a 17-generation phone, and its slower result on newer hardware fits its native top-8 routing and untuned reader set against Edge0's halved expert count and prefetching ([Paging the Experts](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29032)). Flash-MoE's 5.5 tok/s comes from the project's own review document and has not been reproduced, and the 397B run is a stunt whose 2-bit experts raised perplexity from 3.64 to 5.71 and "break JSON/tool calling" ([Anemll flash-moe](https://github.com/Anemll/flash-moe/tree/iOS-App)), which rules out 2-bit for a tutor that returns structured corrections. Swiftlet matters mainly because it passed App Store review inside Priv AI's Experimental Models ([Swiftlet](https://github.com/Avicennasis/Swiftlet); [Priv AI](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/priv-ai/id6765706001)). On English benchmarks streaming costs a few points: Edge0's 35B averaged **79.2 against 83.2** for its full-precision base across five English-centric benchmarks, and the 8B averaged 69.9 against 72.7 ([Edge0 GitHub](https://github.com/Edge0-AI/Edge0)). Neither tier has any multilingual or German result ([edge0.ai](https://edge0.ai/research/edge0)).

Meta's MobileMoE, the other release your question points to, is a different kind of project: a family of small MoEs that sit entirely in RAM, with 1.3–5.3B total and 0.27–0.92B active parameters, 60 experts, top-4 routing and a shared expert ([HF collection](https://huggingface.co/collections/facebook/mobilemoe)). It is fast, with MobileMoE-S decoding at 90–180 tok/s on an iPhone 16 Pro CPU through ExecuTorch, 2.2–3.4× Meta's dense MobileLLM-Pro ([arXiv 2605.27358](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.27358)). It is also **English-only, uses the Llama-3 tokenizer, and carries the gated FAIR Noncommercial license** ([HF MobileMoE-L-QAT](https://huggingface.co/facebook/MobileMoE-L-QAT)), and its fused ExecuTorch kernel has not been released, so it has no role in a German App Store tutor.

### Flash bandwidth and prefill cap every engine

Streaming speed is set by NAND, and iPhone NAND is slow and varies by storage tier. A YouTuber's AnTuTu runs measured **1,653 MB/s** sequential read on a 256 GB iPhone 17 and **3,247 MB/s** on a 256 GB iPhone Air, with 512 GB and 1 TB models scoring higher ([BGR](https://www.bgr.com/1986271/iphone-17-256gb-models-flash-memory-slow-speeds/)), while an M3 Max MacBook Pro reads at 17.5 GB/s ([danveloper/flash-moe](https://github.com/danveloper/flash-moe)). The arithmetic follows directly. Qwen3.6-35B-A3B's 18.1 GB of expert weights across 10,240 experts is about 1.77 MB per expert, so top-8 routing over 40 layers reads roughly 566 MB per token with nothing cached, a ceiling of about **2.8 tok/s at 1.6 GB/s and 5.6 tok/s at 3.2 GB/s**. Edge0's four experts halve that traffic. These ceilings bracket the measured 1.8–3.1 and 6.4 tok/s, and they mean a streamed tutor would run about twice as fast on a learner's 512 GB phone as on a 256 GB one. Caching helps only past a threshold: on the iPhone 17 Pro Max an LRU expert cache hit **0% at 512 MiB** and 47.6% at 1 GiB, because the shortest distinct-expert reuse distance was 312 experts on every trace ([Paging the Experts](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29032)). A useful cache needs well over half a gigabyte, which makes the app's memory ceiling a speed question too.

Prefill is the bigger problem for your app. Edge0 prefills at **4.9–7.2 tok/s** on the iPhone 16 Pro and Routide prefills one token at a time, so a 300-token system prompt plus learner context waits roughly 40–60 seconds before the first word. Edge0's persisted prefix cache covers a fixed system prompt, but each turn's new learner text and coaching context still prefills at single-digit speed. For contrast, MLX Swift decodes Gemma 4 E2B at **49.1 tok/s** on an iPhone 17 Pro ([apple-silicon-llm-bench](https://github.com/john-rocky/apple-silicon-llm-bench)). Energy data is thin: one uncalibrated whole-device estimate put flash-backed decode at **26.5% of the battery per hour** ([Paging the Experts](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29032)). Apple reached the same verdict for its own 20B sparse model. AFM 3 Core Advanced stores its weights in NAND but routes per prompt, "because NAND-to-DRAM bandwidth is too slow to swap weights token by token, as standard MoE models require" ([Apple ML Research](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/introducing-third-generation-of-apple-foundation-models)). Whether third-party apps can call that model, and on which devices, is not documented.

### No streaming engine can carry a custom fine-tune yet

Your current stack runs nearly every MoE architecture but cannot stream any of them. Upstream mlx-swift-lm (3.32.3, released 30 September) ports Qwen3.5-MoE, LFM2-MoE, GraniteMoeHybrid, gpt-oss, OLMoE and more, and its `Gemma4Text` model handles Gemma 4's MoE block ([mlx-swift-lm models](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx-swift-lm/tree/main/Libraries/MLXLLM/Models); [Gemma4Text.swift](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx-swift-lm/blob/main/Libraries/MLXLLM/Models/Gemma4Text.swift)), but it loads every expert into memory MLX owns, all of which counts against the app's budget. The MLX maintainers closed a zero-copy mmap PR in August ("At the moment we are not considering using mmap"), and an mlx-lm PR adding expert offload to disk was closed without review ([mlx #4249](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx/pull/4249); [mlx-lm #1588](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx-lm/pull/1588)). The mmap PR's own measurements show why mmap alone would not have helped: Metal locks an entire no-copy buffer into memory on first use, so a gather that needed one expert pulled in its whole 1.2 GB stacked expert tensor ([mlx #4249](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx/pull/4249)). Real streaming on MLX needs explicit per-expert reads into small buffers, which today exists only outside upstream. SharpAI's SwiftLM `--stream-experts` runs Qwen3.6-35B-A3B at **14.1 tok/s in a 5.7 GB peak, against 48.4 tok/s fully in RAM**, on a 32 GB Mac mini; it has been tested only on Macs and broke on quantized MoEs after an upstream sync ([SwiftLM](https://github.com/SharpAI/SwiftLM)). Anemll's llama.cpp fork lists Gemma4-26B-A4B as stable, also tested only on Macs ([anemll-flash-llama.cpp](https://github.com/Anemll/anemll-flash-llama.cpp)), and BigMoMo, which streams a Gemma 4 MoE, is an Android research system with no code release ([arXiv 2609.14643](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.14643)).

On Edge0, the one iPhone-proven engine, the adapters are what block your fine-tune. The prerouter learns the base model's routing, and the Recover-LoRA imitates the base's full-precision outputs. A German fine-tune changes both the hidden states the prerouter reads and the teacher the Recover-LoRA copies, so both would almost certainly need retraining, a distillation job on a 35B MoE for which the release documents no recipe for a custom base. Edge0 is also sensitive to MLX versions: it pins its Python side to mlx 0.30.6 and warns that older MLX builds produce garbled, mixed-language output on A18 chips ([Edge0 GitHub](https://github.com/Edge0-AI/Edge0)), which your app would have to reconcile with its own mlx-swift 0.31.x. Quantization is not the blocker. At 4-bit, MoEs drift less than dense models: Qwen3.6-35B-A3B under DWQ had a mean KL divergence of **0.027, against 0.113** for the dense Qwen3.6-27B at plain 4-bit ([smcleod.net](https://smcleod.net/2026/04/measuring-model-quantisation-quality-with-kl-divergence/)), provided routers, attention and shared experts keep extra bits ([QuantMoE-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08155v2)), the same discipline your per-layer-embedding (PLE) quantization rule already enforces. The blockers are runtime support and adapter tooling, and you control neither.

## Your own teacher data says active parameters, not total size, limit hard grammar

The public case for a bigger MoE rests on knowledge benchmarks. Gemma 4 26B-A4B scores **86.3 on MMMLU (multilingual MMLU) against 76.6 for E4B**, and 82.6 against 69.4 on MMLU-Pro ([Gemma 4 26B-A4B card](https://huggingface.co/google/gemma-4-26B-A4B-it)), and Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, the base under the 35B streamers, scores 85.2 ([Qwen3.5-35B-A3B card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-35B-A3B)). No published study compares a fine-tuned 26–35B MoE with a fine-tuned dense 4–8B model on grammatical error correction in any language. The nearest evidence is a 2024 T5-based English correction MoE that matched a dense model with three times its active parameters ([arXiv 2410.23507](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.23507)).

Your project has already run the closest real test, and it points the other way. In rounds v2 and v3, `gemma-4-26B-A4B-it` generated your correction data. Asked by name for accusative-instead-of-dative reflexive errors, it produced them in **12%** of rows against **100%** of non-empty rows for the dense Gemma 4 31B, and **26%** of its separable-verb rows were really word-order drills. The students trained on that data scored **42/60 and 46/60** on the core suite, below the untuned model's 48/60 and the Sonnet-taught v1's 54/60. Meanwhile, the same teacher in the same run lifted modal particles 4–5× in the conversation slice (`training/TEACHER_GENERATION_FIX.md`; `training/runpod/bakeoff/README.md`). The failure was specific to constructing subtle grammatical errors, a reasoning task, and your notes name the likely mechanism, about 4B active parameters per token. The project's rule since then has been "dense teachers only."

This is a generation result, not a detection result, so it does not settle how a tuned 26B-A4B would score as a tutor. It is still the most relevant evidence anyone has: on precise German grammar, this MoE behaved like its active size, not its total size. That reading fits your scoreboard's other lessons. The 15-phenomenon corpus *hurt* the ~2B E2B, was absorbed at 2.5B dense and paid off best at 3.4B dense, and "base substrate sets the ceiling" (`training/MODEL_SCOREBOARD.md`; `training/CLAUDE.md`).

Read that way, every on-device MoE route moves in the wrong direction. A streamed 35B-A3B has about 3B active parameters, fewer than the 26B-A4B that already failed your hard cases, and Edge0's switch from eight experts to four cuts the active expert compute further (an inference from the architecture, not a measurement). The MoEs that fit in RAM are worse on this axis. LFM2.5-8B-A1B, Granite-4.0-h-tiny, Ling-3.0-tiny and MobileMoE route through 1–1.5B active parameters, inside the range where your corpus caused interference. The only one with a published multilingual score, LFM2-8B-A1B, reaches **MMMLU 55.3 against E4B's 76.6**, and even in English it trades quality for speed, with MMLU 64.8 against 72.3 for the dense Qwen3-4B ([LFM2-8B-A1B card](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2-8B-A1B)). Its successor publishes no German metrics ([Liquid AI](https://www.liquid.ai/blog/lfm2-5-8b-a1b)), and Ling-3.0-tiny documents no languages ([HF Ling-3.0-tiny](https://huggingface.co/inclusionAI/Ling-3.0-tiny)).

Pruning a large MoE down to phone size does not rescue it either. REAP expert pruning loses about 13% on multiple-choice at 50% compression and produced incoherent output when calibrated on generic text ([REAP](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13999v1)). A community REAP that removed 20% of Gemma 4's experts, calibrated on English text, dropped one knowledge task from 90 to 64 ([0xSero/Gemma-4-21B](https://huggingface.co/0xSero/Gemma-4-21B)). Keeping only the experts German uses would not remove much, because German relies on few language-exclusive experts: masking them in Qwen3-30B-A3B moved accuracy by only −3.2 to +1.6 points ([arXiv 2601.14050](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.14050)).

## The memory ceiling is 6 GB or 8 GB, and E4B sits near the top either way

Your docs and an outside measurement disagree on how much memory the app gets. `docs/MEMORY.md` estimates **~7–8 GB per app on the 12 GB iPhone 17 Pro** and ~4.5–5.5 GB on the 8 GB iPad, against an E4B that holds 4.20 GB of weights, peaks at 4.33 GB on an ordinary turn and reaches 4.99 GB under a very long context. A June 2026 developer-forum report instead measured a flat **~6,144 MB ceiling on both an 8 GB iPhone 16 Pro Max and a 12 GB iPhone 17 Pro Max** with `increased-memory-limit`. Adding `extended-virtual-addressing` changed nothing, and only the debugging entitlement raised it, to ~6,656 MB. The same developer saw Gemma 4 load through mlx-swift-lm at 4.4 GB resident with ~1.7 GB to spare and had to shrink MLX's GPU buffer cache from 512 MB to 64 MB to avoid being killed. Apple had not replied ([Apple Developer Forums 834805](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/834805)).

Flash-MoE's iOS port independently assumes "~6GB of usable app memory" ([IOS_PORT.md](https://github.com/Anemll/flash-moe/blob/iOS-App/FlashMoE-iOS/IOS_PORT.md)), while your scoreboard records that 12B-class models fail to load in practice (`training/MODEL_SCOREBOARD.md`), which fits either figure. Apple publishes no per-device table, the forum measured a Pro Max on an unstated iOS build, and your figure is labelled approximate, so neither should be trusted until it is measured on your devices. The app already logs the number: the `Loading …` row in Settings ▸ Speicher records `MemoryBudget.totalMB`, which is `os_proc_available_memory()` plus `phys_footprint`. Read it in a Release build, since Flash-MoE's port notes that Metal validation in Debug roughly doubles GPU memory. Log `recommendedMaxWorkingSetSize` beside it, because Metal's GPU working set is a second ceiling: a 12 GB iPhone Air reports 8,192 MB, and a 7.32 GB model failed to load there ([Apple Developer Forums 805161](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/805161)).

The answer decides how much room is left, but not whether a better tutor fits. At ~6.1 GB, E4B leaves 1.1–1.8 GB and nothing much above 4.5 GB of weights fits; at 7–8 GB, an 8B-total MoE squeezes in. Neither budget admits a model with a stronger grammar case than E4B.

Bigger hardware is where a bigger tutor could live. Your macOS port already runs MLX, and SwiftLM measured `gemma-4-26b-a4b-it-4bit` (15.3 GB) at **53.9 tok/s** on a 32 GB Mac mini ([SwiftLM](https://github.com/SharpAI/SwiftLM)), with a quantization-aware-trained 4-bit MLX checkpoint already published ([mlx-community](https://huggingface.co/mlx-community/gemma-4-26B-A4B-it-qat-4bit)). Google describes the dense Gemma 4 12B as nearing the 26B MoE at less than half its memory ([Google](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/introducing-gemma-4-12b/)), which, given your dense-over-MoE evidence, makes the 12B the more natural Mac candidate. 16 GB iPads could be a later tier: a 16 GB iPad Pro M4 with both memory entitlements allocated about 16 GB before the compressed-memory limit killed it ([Apple Developer Forums 777370](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/777370)), though that was a malloc test, not a model load. iOS 27 also opens Private Cloud Compute to third-party apps, with a 32K context and a free tier below 2 million first-time downloads, behind a `LanguageModel` protocol that mlx-swift-lm 3.32.3 already bridges ([WWDC26 session 241](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/241/); [mlx-swift-lm releases](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx-swift-lm/releases)). That could handle long stories, but it is Apple's model rather than your tune, and it changes the app's offline and privacy story. Foundation Models adapters remain a poor fit, with an 8K context, ~160 MB adapters retrained for every system-model version, and a toolkit whose last release is "NOT compatible with OS 27+" ([Apple adapter page](https://developer.apple.com/apple-intelligence/foundation-models-adapter/)).

## A memory-mapped E4B could rescue the 8 GB iPad without a new model

The research turned up a gain for the model you already ship. iOS kills apps on `phys_footprint`, and clean file-backed pages, including memory-mapped model weights, are not charged to it ([WWDC18 Session 416](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2018/416); [flutter_gemma #517](https://github.com/DenisovAV/flutter_gemma/issues/517)). The measured effect is large: on an iPhone 17 Pro, llama.cpp ran Gemma 4 E2B with a **191 MB footprint while 2.9 GB of memory-mapped GGUF sat in memory, against 3,010 MB for MLX Swift** ([apple-silicon-llm-bench](https://github.com/john-rocky/apple-silicon-llm-bench)). llama.cpp maps weights with `MAP_SHARED` ([llama-mmap.cpp](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/src/llama-mmap.cpp)), and on macOS Metal locks those page-cache pages in place without copying them ([geistlib #541](https://github.com/geisten/geistlib/issues/541)). MLX copies weights into buffers it owns, which is why all 4.20 GB of E4B's weights count against your budget, and upstream has said it will not change that ([mlx #4249](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx/pull/4249)). Your LiteRT-LM spike saw the same mechanism cut E2B's peak by 0.74 GB on the Mac before its quantizer wrecked the tune (`training/RUNTIME_EXPLORATION.md`).

On the 8 GB iPad this matters more than any new model. E4B's 4.33 GB peak leaves **0.2–1 GB** for everything else, and every crash seen there came from a second heavy resident or a leak (`docs/MEMORY.md`). With ~4.2 GB of weights in the page cache, the kernel would evict and re-read weight pages under pressure instead of killing the app, turning crashes into slow turns. Physical RAM does not grow, so the one-heavy-resident rule should stay; after a picture run, the tutor's next turn would re-read its evicted weights from flash.

The price is a full requalification. Stock Q4_K_M lost ground on Gemma 4 E2B, scoring 76% on GSM8K against 84–91% for the MLX builds, and Google's official QAT q4_0 would not load in llama.cpp at all ([apple-silicon-llm-bench](https://github.com/john-rocky/apple-silicon-llm-bench)). `llama-quantize --tensor-type` can keep individual tensors at q8_0 or f16 ([quantize.cpp](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/tools/quantize/quantize.cpp)), which is the per-tensor control your runtime-exploration closure says any new runtime must prove first; the GGUF names of Gemma 4's PLE tensors still need checking. The same bench found llama.cpp decoding at 38.8 tok/s against MLX's 49.1, drawing **about twice the average power (9.8 W)** and holding only 54% of its burst speed under sustained load.

Some limits remain. Dirty memory still counts, and a react-native-executorch build on an iPhone 17 was killed for running out of free pages during prefill despite file-backed weights ([RNE #1489](https://github.com/software-mansion/react-native-executorch/issues/1489)). A separate mapped-memory ceiling, ~6.5 GiB on an iPhone 13 without entitlements and above 46 GiB with `increased-memory-limit`, can fail a mapping before jetsam acts ([cactus #793](https://github.com/cactus-compute/cactus/issues/793)), so adding `extended-virtual-addressing` is cheap insurance. Apple documents none of how iOS charges mapped pages that Metal has locked in memory, so the evidence is measurement, not specification. llama.cpp ships an official xcframework with each release ([llama.cpp releases](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/releases)), which makes this a second backend behind your session layer rather than a rewrite, but it is still a second backend to maintain.

## Four cheap measurements decide the route

Taken together, your teacher evidence and the memory ceiling sort the realistic options cleanly.

The fine-tune estimate follows Unsloth's guidance: bf16 LoRA with the router frozen, no 4-bit QLoRA for MoEs, and more than 40 GB of VRAM for a 26B-A4B LoRA ([Unsloth faster-moe](https://unsloth.ai/docs/basics/faster-moe); [Unsloth Gemma 4 guide](https://unsloth.ai/docs/models/gemma-4/train)). That means an A100 80 GB at $1.19–1.59/hr or an H100 SXM at $2.69–3.49/hr ([RunPod pricing](https://www.runpod.io/pricing)) for an extrapolated 3–6 hours per round, plus a 150–200 GB volume. If you train on the Mac instead, note that mlx-lm LoRA with no target keys set adapts every linear layer ([mlx-lm #571](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx-lm/issues/571)), and in these MoEs that includes the router.

Streaming belongs on a watch list. Reopen it when one of four things appears: a published recipe for retraining Edge0's prerouter and Recover-LoRA, or an equivalent, on a custom fine-tuned base; expert streaming merged into upstream MLX or mlx-swift-lm; German evaluations of a streamed model; or iPhone prefill fast enough that a tutor turn no longer waits tens of seconds. A one-day sideload of Edge0-8B on the iPhone 17 Pro with your real system prompt would give you your own first-token latency, but it cannot change the verdict while the fine-tune has no way in.

## Conclusion

On iPhones the limit has moved. RAM no longer caps the parameter count, but flash bandwidth, prefill speed and adapter tooling now do, and a solo developer controls none of them. Your own data adds a limit that no engine release will lift: in the one MoE you measured, the quality a German tutor needs, building or spotting a subtle case error, followed active parameters, while the extra total parameters bought fluency. If that holds for detection as it did for generation, streaming a 35B-A3B (about 3B active, fewer under Edge0's routing) is a slower way to get a model no sharper than E4B, and the few-dollar stock evaluation is what turns that suspicion into a measured answer.

The design that would actually suit a narrow tutor is Apple's, not per-token streaming: choose a fixed set of experts once per session and keep them resident. A narrow task concentrates routing on a small subset of experts ([ESFT](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01906v2)), so a grammar tutor is the best case for per-session expert pinning, which would give a large MoE a resident footprint near its active set without paying flash latency on every token. No engine offers that for third-party models today. Until one does, the larger models earn their keep at training time and on bigger hardware, and on the 8 GB iPad the cheapest gain comes from changing how the existing weights are counted against memory, not from adding more of them.
